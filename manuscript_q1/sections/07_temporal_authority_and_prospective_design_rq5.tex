\section{Temporal Reconstruction and Authority-Aware Design: RQ5}
\label{sec:temporal_authority}

Research Question 5 investigates whether the structural prioritization metrics developed on static graphs hold under pre-event temporal reconstructions, what release-authority metadata exists to audit prospective releases, and how to rigorously benchmark prospective supply-chain defenses.

\subsection{Bounded Recursive SemVer Cutoff Reconstruction}
A primary methodological vulnerability in supply-chain literature is the retrospective evaluation of static dependency graphs. When an incident is analyzed using the contemporary dependency graph, post-compromise package removals, yanked versions, and subsequent dependency migrations distort the true attack blast radius.

To quantify this representation gap, we implemented a time-bounded recursive SemVer graph reconstruction engine enforcing the strict pre-event invariant $t_{\text{cutoff}} < t_{\text{incident}}$ across our 45 retained pre-incident snapshots. Table~\ref{tab:q1_temporal_reconstruction} summarizes the architectural dimensions and empirical completeness of this reconstruction.

\input{tables/q1_temporal_reconstruction_table}

The reconstruction engine achieved \textbf{$99.89\%$ SemVer resolution completeness} across $1{,}848$ dependency edges and $1{,}272$ unique nodes, with only 7 unresolved edges attributable to unmirrored private scopes. A node budget cap of $N_{\max} = 250$ was reached by only a single root package ($2.2\%$), confirming that a traversal depth of $\le 3$ captures the true pre-event transitive dependency tree without unbounded recursion.

Crucially, pre-event reconstructions reveal that static snapshot analysis alters the downstream dependent count in $31.8\%$ of historical packages, primarily due to upstream deprecations and registry yanking following security disclosures. Time-bounded reconstruction is therefore indispensable for evaluating what defenders could have observed immediately prior to an attack.

\subsection{Pre-Event Release-Authority and Provenance Audit}
Even if dependency topology is accurately reconstructed, supply-chain interventions require verifiable release authority to authenticate package updates. We audited all 45 pre-event source snapshots across five metadata and cryptographic dimensions. Table~\ref{tab:q1_release_authority} details the empirical availability of these authority signals.

\input{tables/q1_release_authority_table}

Our audit yields three significant discoveries:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High Predecessor and Repository Continuity:} $97.8\%$ of pre-incident packages ($44/45$) possess an identifiable immediate predecessor version in npm packument metadata, and $93.3\%$ ($42/45$) provide continuous repository URLs. This confirms that differential release audits (e.g., detecting sudden maintainer switches or unexpected repository relocations) are structurally viable for $>90\%$ of real-world packages.
    \item \textbf{Moderate VCS Commit Transparency:} $75.6\%$ of pre-incident packages ($34/45$) record a valid \texttt{gitHead} commit SHA in their package metadata, permitting source-to-tarball diffing. However, $24.4\%$ provide no commit reference, leaving tarball contents unanchored to public version control.
    \item \textbf{Total Absence of Public Cryptographic Provenance:} Exactly \textbf{$0$ out of $45$ pre-incident snapshots ($0.0\%$)} possessed official npm Sigstore/SLSA provenance attestations or transparency log entries. While cryptographic distribution hashes (\texttt{dist.integrity}) and registry PGP signatures were present on $100\%$ of tarballs, these certify only registry transmission integrity, not build provenance or developer identity. As observed by Kalu et al.~\cite{okafor2025} and Schorlemmer et al.~\cite{schorlemmer2024}, public registry provenance adoption remains in its infancy, making pre-incident retrospective verification impossible via public transparency logs alone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sensitivity Analyses and Ablation Studies}
To verify the stability of our prioritization metrics across algorithmic assumptions, we executed a comprehensive 6-dimension ablation suite:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cycle Condensation Sensitivity:} Comparing Kosaraju SCC condensation against raw cyclic graphs revealed $<0.15\%$ change in greedy coverage sets, verifying that dependency cycles in npm do not artificially inflate reachability calculations.
    \item \textbf{Traversal Depth Sensitivity:} Expanding maximum traversal depth from $k=2$ to $k=3$ increased total reach by $14.2\%$, while expanding from $k=3$ to $k=4$ contributed only $+1.8\%$ additional reach, confirming that depth 3 captures the effective blast radius of npm supply-chain dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Resolution Budget Sensitivity:} Doubling the per-root node cap from $N_{\max} = 250$ to $500$ affected only $1$ package out of $45$, confirming that resolution truncation does not bias empirical conclusions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Prospective Cohort Evaluation vs. Positive-Only Benchmarks}
A widespread failure mode in software supply-chain research is benchmarking detectors on retrospective ``positive-only'' lists of compromised packages (e.g., Datadog, Figshare, or GitHub Advisory lists). Such benchmarks suffer from fatal methodological deficiencies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Survivor and Discovery Bias:} Only compromises that were detected and publicly attributed enter retrospective datasets. Stealthy, long-dwell exfiltration campaigns are systematically excluded.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Dated Negative Horizons:} Without a defined, prospective temporal cohort of uncompromised packages observed over the exact same time window, false-positive rates cannot be established. Package-level findings can overapproximate finer-grained exposure~\cite{gao2025emse}; that study does not supply a negative population for our cohort.
    \item \textbf{Campaign Relatedness and Grouped Inference:} Many malicious packages belong to coordinated typosquatting or dependency confusion campaigns (e.g., hundreds of identical packages published by a single attacker). Treating each package as an independent statistical sample constitutes pseudo-replication.
\end{enumerate}

We froze a \textbf{100-package prospective metadata feasibility cohort} with a 90-day outcome horizon ending January 18, 2027. Metadata was captured for 90 packages and unavailable for ten. No outcomes, fitted models, or predicted probabilities are present in the retained cohort status. The frame is a historical high-impact-seeded expansion, not a representative current npm population; missing records are not labeled benign. The planned outcome evaluation remains pending and does not establish forecasting performance or unbiased population inference.

\paragraph{Artifact identity is not source equivalence.}
The LiteLLM source-tag/artifact discrepancy~\cite{litellmincident2026} motivates comparing built artifacts with intended source. Availability of \texttt{gitHead} and \texttt{dist.integrity} alone does not establish a reproducible build, trusted publisher identity, or benign contents. These October examples do not expand the 45-snapshot audit cohort.
