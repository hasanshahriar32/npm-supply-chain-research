\section{Introduction}
A software supply-chain review team with five account slots needs to decide which downstream applications its selection will cover. Dependency graphs often collapse package versions to names. When a review team ranks accounts using that representation, the same budget may reach a different set of applications than a ranking that preserves versions. Recent research measures upstream npm leverage and coordinated dependency changes~\cite{samaana2025,he2025}. We study the change in documented coverage by checking pinned application lockfiles.

The decision also depends on the objective. A team may aim to reach more repositories, cover more account-origin routes, or select every observed source for some repositories. Two accounts can reach many of the same repositories while contributing distinct account-origin routes. The graph objectives quantify those differences, and the held-out application comparison tests their representation choices against project-owned evidence.

We compare the objectives on a retained, high-impact-seeded npm graph containing 25,228 identifiers, including bundled contexts, and 12,217 observed usernames. The main endpoint universe contains 7,171 normalized workflow-associated repository keys. The sample represents observed graph reach. We evaluate ranking and greedy-coverage baselines, exploratory scalarization, and an exclusion-oriented heuristic.

We assess the comparison through component benchmarks, a new acquisition and an automated endpoint-evidence audit. Component ablations and small exact benchmarks test whether the exclusion heuristic's additional scoring is useful. A new acquisition from 100 frozen, sampled roots tests transport to a bounded cohort with fresh dependency, maintainer and workflow observations. A stratified probability sample of 120 endpoints supplies commit-pinned evidence about repository mappings and workflow files. Automated indicators characterize this evidence; their accuracy was not manually validated.

We ask five questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How large are the observed coverage, additive-exposure and all-source-exclusion tradeoffs at equal budgets?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do representation choices affect evaluation, and what evidence supports the workflow-associated endpoint observations?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} How sensitive is the exclusion-oriented heuristic to its components, candidate cap and ties, and where does it miss candidate-restricted optima?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Which tradeoffs persist in a new bounded acquisition and a common-population comparison?
\item \textbf{RQ5:} At the same account budget, which held-out applications change documented coverage with the graph representation, and how much uncertainty remains?
\end{itemize}

Our main contribution is a paired empirical comparison of documented downstream coverage under a fixed account budget. Five YunoHost applications switch coverage between the name and version portfolios. The evidence bounds turnover across the full frame, including cases the available files leave unresolved. The additive optimum and standard submodular guarantee supply the theoretical foundations. The simpler exclusion heuristic slightly improves exclusion on the original graph but underperforms the original heuristic in the new cohort. The additional acquisition repeats the coverage--exposure tradeoff while substantially changing the size of representation effects. A separate application pilot measures the feasibility of tracing selections through committed consumer dependencies. Two held-out frames test whether distinct five-account portfolios reach different applications. The separate budget check illustrates where the ranking change matters beyond the frozen five-account contrast.
