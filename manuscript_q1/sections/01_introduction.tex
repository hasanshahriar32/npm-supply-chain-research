\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{odern} software engineering is inextricably bound to open-source package ecosystems. Centralized package managers, led by npm with over 3 million registered packages and billions of weekly downloads, enable rapid assembly of complex applications through deep, transitive dependency graphs~\cite{zimmermann2019}. However, this structural efficiency creates an asymmetric attack surface: an adversary who successfully compromises a single maintainer account can execute malicious code across tens of thousands of downstream continuous-integration (CI) pipelines, server environments, and end-user systems~\cite{ohm2020,koishybayev2022}.

Securing these registry-scale ecosystems requires defensible resource allocation. Ecosystem guardians (such as npm, GitHub, and the Open Source Security Foundation [OpenSSF]) operate under severe capacity constraints: manual source-code audits, hardware security token distribution, mandatory multi-factor authentication (MFA) rollouts, and privileged-access review programs cannot be extended to all 12,000+ active maintainers simultaneously~\cite{schorlemmer2024,okafor2025}. Consequently, defenders rely on algorithmic maintainer prioritization to identify which accounts should receive prioritized controls.

October 2026 disclosures illustrate distinct limits of account-level controls. LiteLLM's final investigation attributes its release compromise to an exposed Agent Platform master key followed by CI secret theft and misuse of multiple developer tokens~\cite{litellm2026}. The published artifacts differed from the official source tag~\cite{litellmincident2026}; this PyPI/container incident motivates our threat model but is not an npm validation case. Pillar's OpenCode disclosure describes a vulnerable legacy npm \texttt{open} invocation on Windows/WSL, fixed on September 28 and disclosed on October 19 without a CVE~\cite{opencode2026}. Railway reported potential environment-variable exposure and a temporary deployment pause~\cite{railway2026,railwaydisclosure2026}. npm's SlugFest trust-binding weakness~\cite{npmtrustedpub2026,npmexpiry2026} and Node.js's High-severity Brotli flaw~\cite{nodejsbrotli2026} further distinguish publishing authorization from runtime safety. These cases motivate sensitivity analyses; they do not establish the efficacy of interventions on our historical graph.

Despite the critical nature of this task, prior prioritization heuristics have operated under unverified assumptions. Commonly deployed heuristics---such as ranking maintainers by total portfolio size, raw download numbers, or greedy unconstrained downstream coverage---treat software dependency graphs as simple reachability networks~\cite{wang2026,liu2022}. In doing so, existing approaches conflate three fundamentally distinct defensive objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Broad Ecosystem Coverage ($C(S)$):} The total number of distinct downstream client repositories that depend on at least one package authored by the selected maintainers. This is a classic submodular set function~\cite{nemhauser1978}.
    \item \textbf{Scenario Exposure Reduction ($H(S)$):} The fraction of maintainer-repository dependency pairs eliminated under a probabilistic model of independent account compromise. Because each account compromise represents a distinct historical event, this is a modular (additive linear) set function.
    \item \textbf{All-Source Exclusion ($P(S)$):} The number of downstream client repositories whose complete set of upstream maintainers is fully contained within the hardened cohort, rendering the client impervious to single-maintainer account takeovers. Because protection requires all upstream maintainers to be selected, this is a supermodular set function.
\end{enumerate}

In this paper, we demonstrate that optimizing for broad coverage ($C$) actively undermines exposure reduction ($H$) and full protection ($P$), creating severe residual exposure across the ecosystem. When defenders maximize greedy coverage, maintainer multi-homing and co-maintenance overlap induce extensive masking: accounts managing ubiquitous shared libraries (e.g., maintainer \texttt{qix} managing \texttt{debug}) are discarded by greedy coverage heuristics because their downstream endpoints are already partially touched by other maintainers, leaving thousands of repositories residually exposed.

Furthermore, we show that standard dependency graph representations introduce massive measurement distortions. Coarse package-name projections---which collapse all historical versions of a package into a single node---inflate downstream attack surfaces by 42.44\% (over 196,000 phantom pairs) and distort individual package blast radii by up to 287\%, misallocating defensive investments.

To address these challenges, this study presents a comprehensive, multi-objective theoretical and empirical investigation of maintainer prioritization across 25,228 npm packages, 12,217 maintainer accounts, and 7,171 workflow repositories. Specifically, we investigate five core Research Questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Objective Trade-offs):} How do distinct coverage $C(S)$ and scenario exposure reduction $H(S)$ diverge under operational budgets, and what is the magnitude of the descriptive trade-off?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Residual Exposure \& Supermodular Protection):} What fraction of covered repositories retain unhardened upstream attack paths under standard heuristics, and can dedicated supermodular optimization maximize all-source exclusion $P(S)$?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Representation Sensitivity):} How does version continuity alter downstream reach closures, maintainer rankings, and cross-representation selection penalties compared to name-collapsed graphs?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Multi-Objective Optimization \& Heterogeneous Risk):} Can a hybrid scalarization $J_\alpha(S)$ achieve a Pareto-optimal frontier between coverage and exposure, and how do heterogeneous maintainer risk models (solo-maintainer bottlenecks, log-portfolio scaling, package-level reach) reshape prioritization?
    \item \textbf{RQ5 (Temporal Reconstruction \& Release Authority):} How effectively can time-bounded SemVer dependency resolution eliminate future-leakage bias, and what pre-event metadata discontinuities characterize real-world compromise snapshots?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
The primary contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Objective Optimization Framework:} We formalize maintainer prioritization across submodular ($C$), modular ($H$), and supermodular ($P$) objectives, proving that single-objective baselines induce extreme polarization.
    \item \textbf{The $\alpha = 0.50$ Pareto Sweet Spot:} We formulate a monotone submodular-modular scalarization $J_\alpha(S)$ that captures $>92\%$ of maximum exposure reduction while maintaining 70\% broad repository coverage.
    \item \textbf{Supermodular Exclusion Breakthrough:} We design a greedy minimum-remaining-source heuristic that breaks past 1,000 fully protected repositories at budget 100 ($P = 1,022$, $+27.8\%$ over greedy, $+91.4\%$ over exact union).
    \item \textbf{Package-Level Sole-Maintainer Sensitivity:} We observe that account-level metrics can miss package-level bottlenecks: weight-aware package-level optimization captures 67.37\% of solo-maintainer exposure mass (+40.05 pp over unweighted union).
    \item \textbf{Residual-Source Threshold Sensitivity:} We evaluate $P_k(S)$ under hypothetical common thresholds, separating structural source counts from unobserved package-level approval policies and multi-token attack roles.
    \item \textbf{Historical SemVer \& Release-Authority Audit:} We evaluate 45 pre-compromise snapshots with 1,848 reconstructed SemVer edges, quantifying that 97.8\% of incidents exhibit predecessor availability suitable for automated release gating.
\end{enumerate}
