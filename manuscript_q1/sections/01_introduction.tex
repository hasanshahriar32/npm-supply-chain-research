\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{N}{pm} dependency and maintainer relations can concentrate downstream reach in a small set of accounts. Existing work establishes maintainer influence, combined reach and the potential value of vetting influential maintainers~\cite{zimmermann2019,zahan2022}. A review program must also specify what a limited account budget should accomplish. Reaching many distinct repositories, removing account-origin exposure, and selecting every observed source are different objectives.

Consider two accounts whose portfolios reach many of the same repositories. After selecting the first, the second may add little distinct coverage while remaining a possible origin in a structural exposure model. An endpoint can therefore be covered while retaining many unselected sources. Mathematical definitions establish that these quantities differ; an empirical study must measure how consequential the distinction is and how observation choices affect the comparison.

We compare the objectives on a retained, high-impact-seeded npm graph containing 25,228 identifiers, including bundled contexts, and 12,217 observed usernames. The main endpoint universe contains 7,171 normalized workflow-associated repository keys. These units are neither a registry-wide sample nor observations of installed-code execution. We evaluate established ranking and greedy-coverage baselines~\cite{zimmermann2019,nemhauser1978}, exploratory scalarization, and an exclusion-oriented heuristic.

We assess the comparison through component benchmarks, a new acquisition and an automated endpoint-evidence audit. Component ablations and small exact benchmarks test whether the exclusion heuristic's additional scoring is useful. A new acquisition from 100 frozen, sampled roots tests transport to a bounded cohort with fresh dependency, maintainer and workflow observations. A stratified probability sample of 120 endpoints supplies commit-pinned evidence about repository mappings and workflow files. Automated indicators characterize this evidence; their accuracy was not manually validated.

We ask four questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How large are the observed coverage, additive-exposure and all-source-exclusion tradeoffs at equal budgets?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do representation choices affect evaluation, and what evidence supports the workflow-associated endpoint observations?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} How sensitive is the exclusion-oriented heuristic to its components, candidate cap and ties, and where does it miss candidate-restricted optima?
\item \textbf{RQ4:} Which tradeoffs persist in a new bounded acquisition and a common-population comparison?
\end{itemize}

Our contribution is measured evidence about these decision and observation choices. The additive optimum and standard submodular guarantee are theoretical foundations, not new coverage algorithms. The heuristic is evaluated without a global-optimality claim; its simpler variant slightly improves exclusion on the original graph but underperforms it in the new cohort. The additional acquisition repeats the coverage--exposure tradeoff while substantially changing the size of representation effects. Extended objective sensitivities and historical reconstruction and metadata-acquisition diagnostics are retained in the appendices rather than presented as operational or forecasting validation.
